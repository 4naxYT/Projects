What You Are Seeing

Every pixel is traced backwards through curved spacetime using the Schwarzschild metric. A virtual photon is fired from your eye into the scene; we track how gravity bends it and determine where it originated. This is backwards raytracing — the standard technique for all physically‑based rendering of black holes, including the one used for the Interstellar visual effects.

The dark silhouette is the black hole shadow — the region from which no light can reach you. The luminous ring around it is the Einstein ring, formed by photons that nearly orbited the singularity before escaping toward you. The glowing disc is the accretion disk: superheated magnetised plasma at millions of Kelvin, now enhanced with spiral density waves and turbulent structure.

How the Raytracer Works

The renderer follows this pipeline for each pixel:

Per‑Pixel Pipeline
1. Cast ray from camera into scene
2. Compute impact parameter b = |camPos × rayDir|
3. If b < b_crit → pixel is shadow (black + ring glow)
4. Look up deflection angle α(b) from precomputed table
5. Rotate ray direction by α around the angular momentum axis
6. Intersect deflected ray with accretion disk (y=0 plane)
7. If no disk hit → sample background star field
8. Apply Doppler beaming, gravitational redshift, tone‑mapping

The deflection table is precomputed at startup using 1000‑step log‑spaced numerical integration of the photon geodesic equation. This replaces what would be ~1000 ODE steps per ray with a single O(1) table lookup + bilinear interpolation — the key optimisation that makes real‑time performance possible.

The Schwarzschild Metric

A non‑rotating, uncharged black hole in General Relativity is described by the Schwarzschild metric — the simplest exact solution to Einstein's field equations (1916):

Spacetime line element
ds² = −(1 − rₛ/r)c²dt²
    + (1 − rₛ/r)⁻¹dr²
    + r²(dθ² + sin²θ dφ²)

Here rₛ = 2GM/c² is the Schwarzschild radius (event horizon). Nothing — not even light — escapes once it crosses rₛ. All simulation lengths are in units of rₛ = 1.

Light Deflection — The Deflection Table

A photon with impact parameter b follows a curved path. The total bending angle is:

Photon deflection integral (exact)
α(b) = 2∫[r₀→∞] dr / (r²√(1/b² − (1−1/r)/r²)) − π

Perihelion r₀ found via
r₀³ − b²·r₀ + b² = 0  (cubic, solved by Newton iteration)

This has no closed form. At startup it is computed at 512 impact parameter samples and stored as a lookup table. Each rendered ray queries this table with bilinear interpolation — giving accurate bending at interactive frame rates. The integration uses 1000 log‑spaced steps from perihelion to r=2000 rₛ.

The table spans b ∈ [2.603, 60]. For b < b_crit ≈ 2.598 the photon spirals into the singularity — those pixels are black. The narrow gap between b_crit and the table start (2.598→2.603) is where the photon ring lives — rays here wrap around the black hole hundreds of times.

Photon Sphere & Shadow
Critical impact parameter
b_crit = 3√3/2 · rₛ ≈ 2.5981 rₛ

Photon sphere radius
r_ph = 1.5 rₛ

Shadow angular radius (distant observer)
θ_sh = arcsin(b_crit / r_obs) ≈ 26.5 µas for M87*

At r = 1.5 rₛ photons can orbit indefinitely — the photon sphere. It is unstable: any perturbation sends the photon spiralling in or escaping. Rays with b just above b_crit are deflected by hundreds of degrees, creating multiple stacked ghost images — the higher‑order Einstein rings.

This shadow was directly measured by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019 (M87*, 6.5 billion M☉) and 2022 (Sgr A*, 4 million M☉), confirming GR to high precision.

Doppler Shift & Relativistic Beaming

Plasma in the accretion disk orbits at Keplerian velocity v = √(GM/r). At the ISCO (3 rₛ) this is ~0.577c. The approaching side is beamed: radiation is blueshifted and boosted in intensity. The receding side is dimmed and redshifted. This asymmetry creates the characteristic bright/dark asymmetry.

Relativistic Doppler factor
D = 1 / [γ(1 − β·cos θ)]
Observed intensity ∝ D³ (specific intensity)
Keplerian: v(r) = √(M/r)  (G=c=1)
Gravitational Redshift

Photons climbing out of a gravitational well lose energy:

Gravitational redshift factor
ν_∞ / ν_emit = √(1 − rₛ/r)

At r = 3 rₛ (ISCO): factor = √(2/3) ≈ 0.816
At r = 1.1 rₛ : factor ≈ 0.302

The colour follows a strict thermal blackbody locus — red → orange → gold → near‑white — with zero blue contribution.

Disk colour + structure
Spiral: sin(n_arms·φ + pitch·ln(r/r_in) + time)
Turbulence: hash‑noise on (r,φ) grid
Inner rim: exponential glow at r ≈ r_in
Colour ramp: R=1.0, G=0.90→0.08, B=0.10→0.00
Relativistic Jets

Many accreting black holes launch relativistic jets — collimated outflows of plasma along the spin axis. While the Schwarzschild metric has no spin, we can visualise a jet‑like structure as a visualisation aid. The jet is modelled as a conical emission region extending from the poles, with exponential brightness falloff from the axis and distance.

Jet model (visualisation)
Cone half‑angle: ~15° from y‑axis
Emission ∝ exp(−d_axis² / σ²) · exp(−|y| / h)
Colour: warm gold‑amber, semi‑transparent
Temporal Anti‑Aliasing (TAA)

The simulation now uses temporal frame accumulation with sub‑pixel jitter. Each frame, the camera projection is offset by a fraction of a pixel using an 8‑point Halton‑inspired jitter sequence. The result is blended with previous frames using exponential moving average (α ≈ 0.12). When the camera moves or settings change, the accumulation buffer resets. Edge AA is automatically disabled when TAA is active to prevent jitter conflicts.

TAA parameters (v6.10)
Jitter pattern: 8‑point quasi‑random sequence
Blend: frameAccum = lerp(frameAccum, newFrame, 0.12)
Reset trigger: camera motion > 0.002 or parameter change
Effective supersampling: ~4‑8× perceived quality improvement
Bloom Post‑Processing

A lightweight bloom effect enhances the visual impact of bright regions (Einstein ring, inner disk). Bright pixels (luminance > 0.55) contribute a small amount of glow to neighbouring pixels via a separable 5×5 approximation, creating the soft halo around the photon ring. Bloom strength and radius are now adjustable.

Planets & Foreground Handling

Planets are now checked with the original undeflected ray first. If a planet is hit and the intersection distance is less than half the camera distance (i.e., the planet is clearly in the foreground), it is rendered without gravitational lensing. Planets behind or near the black hole are rendered with the deflected ray, showing proper lensing distortion. Planets behind the event horizon are correctly occulted.

Coordinate Grid

The overlaid flat‑space grid lies in the equatorial plane (y = 0). Its projected appearance under lensing shows the fish‑eye warping of angular directions near the black hole. Grid line count and thickness are adjustable.

Supersampling & Edge AA

On the sharp boundary between the black shadow and the bright Einstein ring, the image is supersampled with 4 sub‑pixel jittered rays per pixel (RGSS pattern), then averaged with the base sample — giving smooth, aliasing‑free edges. When TAA is enabled, this edge AA is disabled because TAA already provides superior anti‑aliasing.

Schwarzschild vs Kerr Black Holes

The Schwarzschild solution describes a non‑rotating, uncharged black hole. Real astrophysical black holes almost certainly rotate. The Kerr metric (1963) generalises Schwarzschild to include angular momentum J. A rotating black hole drags spacetime around it — frame dragging — and has an ergosphere outside the horizon where nothing can remain stationary relative to distant stars.

Kerr metric key differences
• Two horizons: inner (Cauchy) and outer (event)
• Ring singularity instead of a point
• ISCO depends on spin: r_ISCO = 4.5 rₛ (retrograde) to 1.0 rₛ (prograde, a→1)
• Photon sphere splits into prograde/retrograde orbits
• Shadow is D‑shaped, not circular
• Penrose process can extract energy from the ergosphere

For M87*, the spin is estimated at a ≈ 0.6–0.9 M. The Schwarzschild model captures the essential lensing physics but misses these spin‑dependent asymmetries. A full Kerr raytracer would require a 2D deflection table in (b, spin) and track the Carter constant.

The ISCO — Innermost Stable Circular Orbit

For a non‑spinning black hole, the ISCO lies at r = 3 rₛ. Inside this radius, no stable circular orbit exists — matter spirals rapidly into the horizon. The ISCO marks the inner edge of the standard thin accretion disk. At the ISCO:

ISCO properties (Schwarzschild)
Orbital velocity: v_φ = c/√3 ≈ 0.577c
Specific angular momentum: ℓ = 2√3 GM/c ≈ 3.464 GM/c
Binding energy: E_bind ≈ 0.0572 mc² (energy lost as radiation)
Orbital period: T = 6π√6 GM/c³ ≈ 46.2 GM/c³

The 5.7% binding energy means that matter falling from infinity to the ISCO radiates ~5.7% of its rest mass energy — making accretion one of the most efficient energy sources in the universe (compare to ~0.7% for nuclear fusion). For a maximally spinning Kerr black hole, this efficiency can reach ~42%.

Accretion Disk Physics — Thin Disk Model

The standard Shakura–Sunyaev α‑disk model (1973) describes a geometrically thin, optically thick accretion disk where viscosity transports angular momentum outward and mass inward. The disk is in thermal equilibrium: viscous heating balances radiative cooling at each radius.

Thin disk temperature profile
T(r) ∝ r^(−3/4) · (1 − √(r_in/r))^(1/4)
Peak temperature at r ≈ 1.36 r_in
Typical inner disk T ~ 10⁶–10⁷ K (soft X‑rays)
Outer disk T ~ 10⁴–10⁵ K (UV/optical)

The spectrum is a superposition of blackbody rings at different temperatures, modified by relativistic effects. The observed spectrum from a Schwarzschild black hole is harder (bluer) on the approaching side due to Doppler beaming and softer (redder) on the receding side.

Synchrotron Radiation & the EHT Image

The Event Horizon Telescope images M87* and Sgr A* at 1.3 mm wavelength (230 GHz). At this frequency, the dominant emission mechanism is synchrotron radiation — relativistic electrons spiralling in magnetic fields. The emission is highly polarised, and the EHT measured linear polarisation patterns that trace the magnetic field geometry near the horizon.

Synchrotron emissivity
j_ν ∝ n_e · B^(p+1)/2 · ν^(−(p−1)/2)
where p ≈ 2–3 is the electron energy spectral index
Polarisation fraction: up to ~70% for uniform B‑field

The EHT's polarisation maps revealed ordered magnetic fields in the accretion flow, consistent with magnetically arrested disk (MAD) models where strong poloidal fields thread the horizon and launch jets via the Blandford–Znajek process.

The Blandford–Znajek Jet Mechanism

Relativistic jets are powered by spin energy extraction from the black hole. The Blandford–Znajek (BZ) process (1977) uses the black hole's rotation to twist magnetic field lines, creating a Poynting flux that accelerates plasma to relativistic speeds along the spin axis.

BZ jet power scaling
P_BZ ∝ B² · a² · M²
where B is the horizon magnetic field strength
a = J/M is the dimensionless spin parameter
Typical jet power: 10⁴²–10⁴⁵ erg/s (for supermassive BHs)

M87's jet extends over 5000 light‑years and is visible from radio to X‑ray wavelengths. The jet base is unresolved even by the EHT, but its launch point lies within a few gravitational radii of the horizon. The jet is one‑sided at small scales due to relativistic Doppler boosting of the approaching side.

Gravitational Lensing Formalism

The bending of light by gravity is the foundation of this simulation. In the Schwarzschild metric, the deflection angle for a photon with impact parameter b is:

Exact bending angle (elliptic integral form)
α(b) = 4√(r₀/(r₀−2M)) · F(φ₀, k) − π
where F is the incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind
k² = (r₀−2M)/(3r₀−2M) for the Schwarzschild case

For large b (weak field), the deflection reduces to the classical Einstein formula: α ≈ 4GM/(bc²) = 2rₛ/b. The weak‑field approximation is accurate to <1% for b > 20 rₛ. For b near b_crit, the deflection diverges logarithmically as the photon approaches the photon sphere.

Einstein Rings & Multiple Images

When a light source lies directly behind the black hole (or very close to the line of sight), gravitational lensing can produce Einstein rings — circular images of the source. For a point source perfectly aligned behind a Schwarzschild black hole:

Einstein ring angular radius
θ_E ≈ √(4GM/(c²) · D_ls/(D_l·D_s))
where D_l, D_s, D_ls are angular diameter distances

A source slightly off‑axis produces multiple images: one on each side of the lens, with the brighter image on the side closer to the lens centre. In the strong‑field regime, an infinite sequence of higher‑order Einstein rings (photon rings) converges toward the critical curve at b = b_crit. The n‑th ring corresponds to photons that have completed n half‑orbits around the black hole.

The Photon Ring — Nature's Finest Ruler

The photon ring is the bright, narrow annulus just outside the black hole shadow. It is composed of photons that have orbited the black hole 1, 2, 3... times before reaching the observer. Each successive sub‑ring is exponentially demagnified by a factor ~e^π ≈ 23 and delayed by ~16 GM/c³ (~8 hours for M87*).

Photon ring properties
n=1 ring: ~50% of total photon ring flux
n=2 ring: ~2% of flux, width ~0.1 µas for M87*
n=∞ (critical curve): infinitesimal width, zero flux
Ring diameter ∝ 1/r_obs (diverges as observer approaches BH)

The photon ring is a universal feature of black hole spacetimes. Its shape is determined purely by the metric and is independent of the accretion flow details. Measuring the photon ring shape is a key scientific goal for next‑generation EHT observations (ngEHT) and space‑VLBI missions.

Black Hole Thermodynamics

In the 1970s, Bekenstein and Hawking showed that black holes obey laws analogous to thermodynamics. The four laws of black hole mechanics are:

The Four Laws
0th: Surface gravity κ is constant on the horizon (thermal equilibrium)
1st: dM = (κ/8πG)dA + Ω_H dJ (energy conservation)
2nd: Horizon area A never decreases: dA ≥ 0 (entropy increase)
3rd: κ cannot be reduced to zero (absolute zero unreachable)

The entropy of a black hole is S = k_B·A/(4ℓ_P²) where ℓ_P is the Planck length. For M87*, S ≈ 10⁹⁰ k_B — vastly exceeding the entropy of all other objects in the observable universe combined. The temperature is T_H = ħκ/(2πk_B·c), about 10⁻¹⁷ K for a solar‑mass black hole (colder than the CMB).

Hawking Radiation

In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes are not truly black — quantum effects near the horizon cause them to emit thermal radiation at temperature T_H. This is a consequence of quantum field theory in curved spacetime, not a full theory of quantum gravity.

Hawking temperature & lifetime
T_H = ħc³/(8πGM k_B) ≈ 6.17×10⁻⁸(M⊙/M) K
Evaporation time: τ ≈ 2.1×10⁶⁷ (M/M⊙)³ years
For M87*: T_H ≈ 10⁻¹⁷ K, τ ≈ 10⁹⁹ years (negligible)

Hawking radiation is undetectable for astrophysical black holes (the CMB at 2.7 K is vastly hotter). Only primordial black holes with M < 10¹⁵ g (~asteroid mass) would have evaporated by now and could produce observable gamma‑ray bursts. No such events have been confirmed.

The Information Paradox

Hawking's discovery led to the black hole information paradox: if a black hole forms from pure quantum states and evaporates into thermal radiation, information appears to be destroyed — violating unitarity, a fundamental principle of quantum mechanics.

Proposed resolutions
• Information encoded in Hawking radiation correlations (Page curve)
• Information stored on a "stretched horizon" (holographic principle)
• Remnants: evaporation stops at Planck scale, storing information
• Firewalls: high‑energy barrier at horizon destroys infalling observers
• ER=EPR: entanglement is geometrised via wormholes

Recent work using the holographic principle and AdS/CFT correspondence suggests information is preserved and encoded in subtle correlations of the Hawking radiation. The debate continues to drive fundamental research in quantum gravity.

The No‑Hair Theorem

A stationary black hole in General Relativity is completely characterised by only three parameters: mass M, angular momentum J, and electric charge Q. All other information about the matter that formed it is lost — the black hole has "no hair."

The three hairs
Schwarzschild: M only (static, uncharged)
Reissner–Nordström: M, Q (static, charged)
Kerr: M, J (rotating, uncharged)
Kerr–Newman: M, J, Q (rotating, charged) — most general

Astrophysical black holes are expected to have negligible charge (they would quickly neutralise by accreting opposite charges), so the Kerr metric is the relevant description. The no‑hair theorem has been tested observationally: the EHT shadow measurements are consistent with Kerr and constrain deviations.

Cosmic Censorship Hypothesis

Roger Penrose proposed that naked singularities — singularities not hidden behind an event horizon — cannot form from generic initial conditions in classical General Relativity. This is the weak cosmic censorship hypothesis. If true, all singularities produced by gravitational collapse are clothed by horizons.

Naked singularity implications
• Causality breakdown (closed timelike curves possible)
• Predictability lost — GR cannot determine future evolution
• Quantum gravity effects become globally visible
• Violates the "deterministic" nature of classical GR

While no naked singularity has been observed, the hypothesis remains unproven. Counter‑examples exist in highly idealised scenarios, but whether they can form in realistic collapse is an open question. If naked singularities exist, they would provide a window into quantum gravity.

Gravitational Waves from Black Hole Mergers

When two black holes merge, they emit gravitational waves — ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein (1916) and first directly detected by LIGO in 2015 (GW150914). The signal has three phases:

Merger waveform phases
Inspiral: gradual energy loss, chirp‑like frequency increase
Merger: horizons touch, peak luminosity ~10⁵⁶ erg/s
Ringdown: final black hole settles via quasinormal modes
QNM frequencies depend only on M, J (no‑hair test!)

The quasinormal modes (QNMs) of the ringdown are the "sound" of the black hole — damped sinusoids with complex frequencies ω = ω_R + iω_I. Measuring multiple QNM frequencies provides a stringent test of the no‑hair theorem. Future detectors (LISA, Einstein Telescope) will measure ringdowns with exquisite precision.

Tidal Forces & Spaghettification

An object falling into a black hole experiences tidal forces — the difference in gravitational acceleration between its near and far sides. Near the horizon, these forces diverge. For a stellar‑mass black hole (~10 M⊙), the tidal force at the horizon is ~10⁹ g/cm — an astronaut would be stretched into a thin strand of atoms (spaghettification) well before reaching the horizon.

Tidal acceleration scaling
Δa ≈ 2GMΔr/r³
At ISCO (r=3rₛ): Δa ≈ 0.074·(c²/rₛ)·(Δr/rₛ)
For M87* (6.5×10⁹ M⊙): tidal force at horizon is ~10⁻⁶ g/cm — survivable!

Paradoxically, supermassive black holes have gentler tidal forces at their horizons because the Schwarzschild radius scales linearly with mass while tidal forces scale as M/r³ ∝ 1/M². You could cross the event horizon of M87* without immediately noticing — though you would still be causally trapped forever.

The Eddington Luminosity Limit

Accretion is self‑limiting. When the radiation pressure from the disk's luminosity equals the inward gravitational force on infalling matter, accretion stalls. This defines the Eddington luminosity:

Eddington luminosity
L_Edd = 4πGM m_p c / σ_T ≈ 1.26×10³⁸ (M/M⊙) erg/s
For M87*: L_Edd ≈ 8×10⁴⁷ erg/s
Eddington accretion rate: ṁ_Edd = L_Edd/(ηc²) ≈ 2.2×10⁻⁸(M/M⊙) M⊙/yr

Many black holes accrete at sub‑Eddington rates (0.01–0.1 L_Edd), producing thin disks. Super‑Eddington accretion (exceeding L_Edd) is possible if the disk is geometrically thick ("slim disk") or if photon bubbles reduce the effective opacity. Ultraluminous X‑ray sources (ULXs) may be stellar‑mass black holes accreting at super‑Eddington rates.

The M–σ Relation

Observations reveal a tight correlation between the mass of a supermassive black hole and the velocity dispersion σ of stars in its host galaxy's bulge:

M–σ relation (empirical)
M_BH ≈ 10⁸ (σ/200 km/s)^4 M⊙
Scatter: ~0.3 dex (factor of 2)
Holds from ~10⁶ to ~10¹⁰ M⊙

This correlation suggests co‑evolution of black holes and their host galaxies, likely mediated by AGN feedback: the black hole's energy output regulates star formation by heating or expelling gas. Understanding this feedback is a major goal of extragalactic astrophysics.

The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)

The EHT is a global very‑long‑baseline interferometry (VLBI) array operating at 230 GHz (1.3 mm). By combining radio telescopes across the Earth, it achieves an angular resolution of ~20 µas — sharp enough to resolve the shadow of M87* and Sgr A*.

EHT key specifications
Frequency: 230 GHz (λ = 1.3 mm)
Resolution: ~20 µas (Earth‑diameter baseline)
Sites: 8 telescopes (2017), 11+ (2022+), including ALMA, SMA, SPT
Sensitivity: ~10 µJy (with ALMA phasing)
Targets: M87* (6.5×10⁹ M⊙), Sgr A* (4.15×10⁶ M⊙)

The EHT does not take direct images — it measures Fourier components (visibilities) of the source brightness distribution. Images are reconstructed using algorithms like CLEAN and regularised maximum likelihood (RML). The 2019 M87* image was consistent with a Kerr black hole shadow and ruled out many alternative compact objects.

Quasinormal Modes & Black Hole Spectroscopy

After a merger, the remnant black hole vibrates at specific quasinormal mode frequencies. These damped oscillations are the "ringing" of spacetime itself. Each mode is characterised by:

QNM parameters (Schwarzschild, ℓ=2, m=0)
Fundamental mode: ω₂₀₀ ≈ 0.3737 - 0.08896i (GM/c³ units)
Frequency: f ≈ 1.2 kHz (10 M⊙) or 12 mHz (10⁶ M⊙)
Damping time: τ ≈ 55 µs (10 M⊙) or 5.5 s (10⁶ M⊙)
Overtones decay faster: ω₂₀₁, ω₂₀₂, ...

Detecting multiple QNM frequencies from a single merger would provide a unique "spectroscopic" test of the no‑hair theorem — confirming that the remnant is indeed a Kerr black hole described only by M and J. This is a key science goal for next‑generation ground‑based detectors.

The Penrose Process

In a rotating (Kerr) black hole, the ergosphere — a region outside the horizon where spacetime is dragged faster than light relative to distant stars — allows energy extraction. A particle falling into the ergosphere can split into two: one falls into the horizon with negative energy (relative to infinity), while the other escapes with more energy than the original particle.

Penrose process energy gain
Maximum efficiency: ~20.7% of mc² (extreme Kerr, a→1)
Requires relativistic breakup velocities (~0.5c)
Magnetic variant: Blandford–Znajek (more astrophysically relevant)

The Penrose process is a beautiful demonstration of how rotational energy can be extracted from a black hole. The BZ mechanism is essentially its electromagnetic, field‑theoretic generalisation — and is likely responsible for powering relativistic jets.

White Holes & Wormholes

The maximally extended Schwarzschild solution contains not just a black hole but also a white hole — a time‑reversed black hole from which matter and light can only escape — and an Einstein–Rosen bridge (wormhole) connecting two asymptotically flat universes.

Status of white holes & wormholes
• White holes: mathematically valid, but likely non‑physical
• Would require fine‑tuned initial conditions at the Big Bang
• Einstein–Rosen bridge: non‑traversable, pinches off instantly
• Traversable wormholes require exotic matter (negative energy)
• No observational evidence for either

While fascinating theoretically, white holes and macroscopic wormholes are not expected to exist in our universe. The realistic gravitational collapse of a star produces only the black hole region of the extended solution — the white hole and wormhole are "covered" by the collapsing matter.

Black Hole Types — A Population Census

Black holes are observed across an enormous range of masses, from ~5 M⊙ to >10¹⁰ M⊙:

Known black hole populations
Stellar‑mass: 5–100 M⊙ (X‑ray binaries, ~50 confirmed in Milky Way)
Intermediate‑mass (IMBHs): 100–10⁵ M⊙ (ULXs, globular clusters, ~10 candidates)
Supermassive (SMBHs): 10⁶–10¹⁰ M⊙ (galactic nuclei, ubiquitous)
Primordial (PBHs): 10⁻¹⁸–10¹⁵ M⊙ (hypothetical, dark matter candidate)
Total Milky Way stellar BHs: estimated ~10⁸ (from IMF)

The origin of SMBHs is a major puzzle. How did ~10⁹ M⊙ black holes exist just 700 million years after the Big Bang (observed as quasars at z > 7)? Scenarios include direct collapse of primordial gas clouds, rapid accretion onto stellar‑mass seeds, or hierarchical mergers in dense star clusters.

Numerical Relativity & Ray Tracing

Simulating black hole spacetimes requires solving Einstein's equations numerically. Numerical relativity codes evolve the full 4D spacetime on supercomputers, enabling predictions of gravitational waveforms and accretion dynamics. Ray tracing in these dynamic spacetimes is computationally intensive.

State‑of‑the‑art GR ray tracing codes
• GRMHD: HARM, BHAC, GRHydro (magnetohydrodynamics + GR)
• Ray tracing: GRTRANS, IPOLE, BHOSS, Odyssey
• Image synthesis: ipole, BHOSS‑img, RAPTOR
• GPU‑accelerated: GRay, ODYSSEY‑GPU

This simulation uses a much simpler approach — analytic spacetime + precomputed deflection table — trading full dynamical accuracy for real‑time interactivity. Yet the essential lensing features (shadow, photon ring, Einstein rings) are captured faithfully because they depend only on the static metric geometry.

The Interstellar Connection

The 2014 film Interstellar featured the most accurate black hole visualisation ever created at the time. The VFX team at Double Negative, collaborating with Kip Thorne, developed DNGR — a general‑relativistic ray tracing code that rendered the now‑iconic image of "Gargantua."

Interstellar vs this simulation
DNGR: Full Kerr metric, 1 billion+ rays, hours per frame
This sim: Schwarzschild metric, 800×485 pixels, real‑time
Both use backwards ray tracing with deflection integration
DNGR discovered the nested photon ring structure by accident

Thorne's collaboration with Double Negative led to a scientific paper (James et al., 2015, CQG) describing the rendering techniques. The film's visualisation helped popularise accurate black hole imagery and inspired citizen‑science and educational simulations like this one.

The entire per‑pixel relativistic computation reduces to: one cross product (for b), one table lookup + lerp (for α), one rotation, and one plane intersection. The rest is classical graphics — tone mapping, bloom, and compositing.
